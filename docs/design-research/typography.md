# Typography & Type Systems

> Typography is the visual arrangement of language — the discipline of selecting, sizing, spacing, and combining letterforms so that text communicates with maximum clarity and appropriate character.

## Why it matters

Every UI is primarily text. The decisions made about typeface, scale, spacing, and hierarchy determine whether a user reads confidently or struggles, trusts the product or doubts it, completes a form or abandons it. Poor typography is the single most common source of "AI slop" aesthetics in generated interfaces: default system fonts at default sizes with no considered scale, no rhythm, and no voice. Conversely, a well-constructed type system — even using only one typeface family — is the fastest path from a wireframe to something that reads as deliberate and designed.

## Core principles

**1. Anatomy determines pairing potential.**
Every typeface is built from measurable structural features: x-height (height of lowercase letters relative to caps), contrast (ratio between thick and thin strokes), stress axis (angle of a curved stroke's thinnest point), serif style, and aperture (openness of counters in letters like *c* and *e*). Typefaces with similar x-heights and stress axes share an inner logic that makes them readable together. Bringhurst's instruction is direct: "Pair serifed and unserifed faces on the basis of their inner structure." [Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 4th ed., book, no canonical URL]

**2. Classification gives you a shared vocabulary.**
The Vox-ATypI system — devised by Maximilien Vox in 1954, adopted by the Association Typographique Internationale in 1962, and de-adopted in 2021 as inadequate for non-Latin scripts — organizes Latin typefaces into nine historical classes: Humanes (Venetian), Garaldes (Garamond-era oldstyle), Réales (transitional), Didones (high-contrast modern), Mécanes (slab serif), Linéales (sans-serif), Incises, Manuaires, and Scriptes. [Vox-ATypI classification, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vox-ATypI_classification] For practical UI work, five buckets cover most decisions: **serif** (stroke contrast, bracketed serifs — suited to long reading at body sizes), **sans-serif** (geometric, humanist, or grotesque — suited to UI labels, headings, captions), **slab/Mécane** (uniform-thickness serifs — strong at display sizes, sturdy at body), **monospace** (fixed-width — code, tabular data), **display** (expressive, narrow use at large sizes only).

**3. Pairing requires contrast and kinship simultaneously.**
A pair that is too similar reads as a mistake; a pair that is too different reads as chaos. The classic rule: pair a serif body with a structurally related sans-serif heading, or vice versa — the visual contrast between category is clear but the shared proportions keep harmony. Superfamilies eliminate the problem entirely: type families such as Roboto/Roboto Slab/Roboto Mono, or Source Serif/Source Sans/Source Code Pro, are designed with matching metrics so any combination is pre-validated. [99designs, "3 principles you need to pair typefaces perfectly", https://99designs.com/blog/tips/typeface-pairing-principles/] When mood matters — a warm humanist sans like Inter can pair with a humanist oldstyle like Freight Text because they share organic, hand-drawn origins even across categories.

**4. Modular scales impose proportional discipline.**
A type scale is a sequence of sizes generated by multiplying a base size by a fixed ratio. Common ratios and their musical analogues: **Major Second (1.125)** — subtle, suitable for data-dense UIs; **Major Third (1.25)** — moderate, clear level differentiation, good for product UIs; **Perfect Fourth (1.333)** — distinct hierarchy, appropriate for editorial or content sites; **Perfect Fifth (1.5)** — dramatic, heading-heavy designs only; **Golden Ratio (1.618)** — extreme contrast, rarely suitable for more than three levels. With a 16px base and a 1.333 (Perfect Fourth) ratio the scale yields: 12 / 16 / 21 / 28 / 37 / 50px — roughly matching the six heading levels. [modularscale.com, https://www.modularscale.com/; A List Apart, "More Meaningful Typography", https://alistapart.com/article/more-meaningful-typography/]

**5. Measure (line length) controls reading comfort.**
The measure is the width of a column of text. Too wide, and the eye loses its place returning to the start of the next line. Too narrow, and the eye makes too many trips, disrupting comprehension. Butterick's rule: **45–90 characters including spaces** for body text. The practical sweet spot is 55–75 characters. An empirical check: two to three lowercase alphabets should fit on one line (`abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz` × 2.3 ≈ 60 chars). [Butterick, *Practical Typography*, https://practicaltypography.com/line-length.html] In CSS, `max-width: 70ch` on a prose container is a direct and accurate implementation.

**6. Leading (line-height) is the vertical rhythm of text.**
Leading is the vertical distance from one baseline to the next, expressed as a multiplier of the font size. Too tight: lines blur together and the eye has nowhere to rest. Too loose: the text loses cohesion. Butterick recommends **120–145% of the point size** (i.e., `line-height: 1.2–1.45`). For web body text, the accepted range is **1.4–1.6**, with 1.5 widely cited as the baseline default. [Butterick, *Practical Typography*, https://practicaltypography.com/summary-of-key-rules.html] Sans-serif typefaces generally need more leading than serifs because they have lower contrast and less visible baseline rhythm. Longer measures also require more leading to help the eye return accurately.

**7. Tracking (letter-spacing) is not decoration — it has rules.**
Normal-weight lowercase text at reading sizes should never be tracked. "Lowercase letters don't ordinarily need letterspacing" — adding it stretches words into ambiguous shapes. [Butterick, *Practical Typography*, https://practicaltypography.com/letterspacing.html] The exceptions are explicit: **all-caps and small-caps text should be tracked 5–12% (0.05–0.12em)** — capitals were designed to sit next to lowercase letters and appear cramped in solid-caps setting without compensation. Large display headings (60px+) often benefit from slight negative tracking (−0.01 to −0.03em) to compensate for optical loosening at scale. CSS: `letter-spacing: 0.08em` on a `.caps` class; `letter-spacing: -0.02em` on a hero heading.

**8. Hierarchy is built from a small set of variables — use each sparingly.**
Visual hierarchy signals importance without the reader consciously processing it. The available levers are: **size** (most powerful — a 2× size jump is unambiguous), **weight** (bold vs. regular within the same typeface), **color/value** (reduced opacity or secondary color for supporting text), **case** (uppercase labels read as category, not content), and **spacing** (breathing room around a title elevates it). The key constraint: changing only one variable at a time between hierarchy levels reads as system. Changing three simultaneously reads as accident. Smashing Magazine's analysis of hierarchy systems recommends three distinct levels for most interfaces (primary, secondary, tertiary) and warns that six or more weights/sizes applied simultaneously destroy the contrast that hierarchy depends on. [Smashing Magazine, "Typographic Hierarchies", https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/10/typographic-hierarchies/]

**9. Readability is not legibility — both matter but differently.**
**Legibility** is the ease with which individual characters are distinguished from one another — a property of the typeface design itself (open apertures, clear differentiation between similar glyphs like l/I/1). **Readability** is the ease with which extended text can be understood — a property of the typesetting (size, measure, leading, contrast). Nielsen Norman Group research on glanceable text found that condensed typeface widths required **11.2% more reading time** than regular widths, and lowercase required **26% more time** than uppercase for isolated-word scanning. [NN/g, "Typography for Glanceable Reading: Bigger Is Better", https://www.nngroup.com/articles/glanceable-fonts/] Note the context dependency: these findings apply to glanceable labels (UI buttons, notifications, wayfinding) — not body paragraphs, where uppercase reading is actually slower.

**10. Web font loading must be explicitly managed.**
When a custom font is declared but not yet downloaded, browsers take one of two default paths: **FOIT** (Flash of Invisible Text — text is hidden until the font loads, blocking content) or **FOUT** (Flash of Unstyled Text — text renders in the fallback, then swaps to the web font, causing a layout shift). The `font-display` descriptor controls this: `swap` shows fallback immediately (FOUT, infinite swap period); `optional` blocks for at most 100ms and uses the font only if cached (best for Core Web Vitals); `fallback` is a compromise (100ms block, 3-second swap window). For most product UIs: `font-display: swap` plus `<link rel="preload" as="font" crossorigin>` for the critical weight. [web.dev, "Best practices for fonts", https://web.dev/articles/font-best-practices; web.dev, "Optimize web fonts", https://web.dev/learn/performance/optimize-web-fonts]

**11. Variable fonts consolidate weight/width/style into one file.**
A variable font is a single `.woff2` file containing a continuous design space across one or more axes: `wght` (weight, 100–900), `wdth` (width, 25–200), `ital` (italic, 0–1), `slnt` (slant), and `opsz` (optical size). A family that previously required six separate static files (Thin, Regular, Medium, SemiBold, Bold, ExtraBold) can be delivered as one variable file. Web.dev reports that one variable file replacing 48 individual fonts achieved an 88% file-size reduction. Subsetting (stripping unused glyphs via `unicode-range`) remains important regardless. WOFF2 format only — it offers up to 30% better compression than WOFF and has universal modern browser support. [web.dev, "Introduction to variable fonts on the web", https://web.dev/articles/variable-fonts; web.dev, "Best practices for fonts", https://web.dev/articles/font-best-practices]

**12. Fluid type with `clamp()` eliminates brittle breakpoints.**
`clamp(min, preferred, max)` scales a value linearly between a minimum and maximum as the viewport grows. The preferred value combines a viewport-width component and a relative offset: `font-size: clamp(1rem, 2vw + 0.5rem, 1.5rem)`. To calculate: given a min size, max size, and the viewport range over which scaling should occur, use `v = 100 × (maxSize − minSize) / (maxViewport − minViewport)` and `r = (minViewport × maxSize − maxViewport × minSize) / (minViewport − maxViewport)`. Accessibility constraint: use `rem` for the min and max bounds (not `px`) so that the user's browser font-size preference is respected. A viewport-only value such as `font-size: 2vw` with no `rem` clamp violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text). [Smashing Magazine, "Modern Fluid Typography Using CSS Clamp", https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/01/modern-fluid-typography-css-clamp/; Smashing Magazine, "Addressing Accessibility Concerns With Using Fluid Type", https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2023/11/addressing-accessibility-concerns-fluid-type/]

**13. Optical sizing adapts letterforms to their rendering size.**
Display type (60px+) can afford high contrast between thick and thin strokes, tight spacing, and delicate details — but these same features make text illegible at small sizes. The `opsz` variable font axis encodes multiple optical cuts in a single file: at small sizes it increases stroke weight, opens spacing, and raises x-height; at large sizes it recovers the delicate proportions. CSS exposes this via `font-optical-sizing: auto` (the default, enabled for all variable fonts with an opsz axis) or `font-variation-settings: "opsz" 14`. This replicates what print type designers have done for centuries with different physical sizes of metal type — sizes below 12pt were cut differently from display sizes above 36pt. [MDN, "font-optical-sizing", https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-optical-sizing; Google Fonts Knowledge, "Optical Size axis (opsz)", https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/glossary/optical_size_axis]

## How to apply (web UI)

- **DO** set `max-width: 65ch` (or 60–75ch) on prose containers to enforce readable line length.
- **DO** use `line-height: 1.5` for body text as a floor; increase to 1.6–1.7 for longer measures or small text (below 15px).
- **DO** use `letter-spacing: 0.06em` to `0.1em` on any element using `text-transform: uppercase`.
- **DO** set negative tracking (`letter-spacing: -0.02em`) on hero headings above 48px to tighten optical spacing.
- **DO** define a modular scale at project start — Major Third (1.25) for UI-dense apps, Perfect Fourth (1.333) for content-first sites.
- **DO** use `font-size: clamp(1rem, 2.5vw + 0.75rem, 1.25rem)` (adjust numbers to taste) for responsive body text, with `rem` bounds.
- **DO** add `font-display: swap` to all `@font-face` declarations; preload the primary weight with `<link rel="preload" as="font" crossorigin>`.
- **DO** serve WOFF2 only; drop WOFF, TTF, and EOT.
- **DO** prefer variable fonts when using 3+ weights of a family; use `font-variation-settings` or high-level properties (`font-weight`, `font-stretch`).
- **DO** allow `font-optical-sizing: auto` to remain set (the default); only override with `none` for intentional design effects.
- **AVOID** applying `letter-spacing` to lowercase body text — it fractures word shapes and slows reading.
- **AVOID** setting `line-height` below 1.4 on any text meant for sustained reading.
- **AVOID** using more than two typeface families in one UI unless you have an explicit editorial reason and the contrast is deliberate.
- **AVOID** `font-size` below `0.875rem` (14px) for any body copy; below `0.75rem` (12px) for any rendered text at all.
- **AVOID** bare `vw` units for font-size without a `clamp()` minimum — text will become unreadably small on narrow viewports.

## Anti-patterns

**Uncontrolled measure.** Text that flows edge-to-edge in a browser window at 1400px produces 160+ character lines. This is the single most common typographic error in generated UIs. Always constrain prose width.

**Default line-height with small text.** Browser default `line-height: normal` is approximately 1.2, which is appropriate for headings but creates cramped, hard-to-read paragraphs. It appears on body text when developers forget to set it explicitly.

**Tracking lowercase body copy.** Positive `letter-spacing` on paragraph text is a persistent "design-flavoring" mistake — it feels like it adds sophistication but it measurably slows reading and is flagged as incorrect by every major typography authority.

**Too many hierarchy levels.** Using six different font sizes, four weights, and three colors simultaneously destroys the contrast hierarchy depends on. Three levels (primary/secondary/tertiary) serve most interfaces; four is a justified maximum.

**Font loading without `font-display`.** Omitting `font-display` defaults to browser-defined behavior, which in Chrome is equivalent to `block` — text is invisible for up to 3 seconds on slow connections. This is both a performance and accessibility failure.

**Mixing typefaces without structural relationship.** Pairing two typefaces that share the same mood but have incompatible x-heights or stress axes creates unresolvable visual friction. Superfamily pairings or verified pairings from a tool like Fontjoy eliminate this risk.

**Fluid type without accessible bounds.** `font-size: calc(1vw + 0.5rem)` with no maximum will grow indefinitely. `font-size: 2vw` with no minimum violates WCAG 1.4.4. Always use `clamp()` with `rem` bounds.

**Ignoring optical sizing.** Setting a variable font's weight to a value appropriate for body text and using the same letterform at 72px display size results in over-contrasted, delicate-looking display type. Set `font-variation-settings: "opsz" 72` or rely on `font-optical-sizing: auto` with a font that supports it.
