# Color Theory & Palettes for UI

> The disciplined use of hue, lightness, and chroma to communicate hierarchy, meaning, and brand while meeting perceptual and legal accessibility standards.

## Why it matters

Color is the fastest signal a UI can send — it registers before text is read. A palette built without a principled system produces inconsistent contrast, brittle dark-mode inversions, and barriers for the roughly 8% of men who have some form of color vision deficiency. Conversely, a well-constructed palette based on perceptual color models can be generated algorithmically, tokens can be swapped globally, and accessibility checks become predictable rather than ad-hoc [Material Design Dark Theme; WCAG 2.1; evilmartians OKLCH].

## Core principles

**1. Color models encode different things — choose the right one for the job.**
RGB and hex are hardware-native: they describe phosphor intensities, not perceptual experience. HSL is cylindrical and human-readable, but its lightness channel is mathematically broken — yellow at HSL `50% L` looks dramatically lighter than blue at the same value [Lea Verou, "LCH colors in CSS," 2020]. LAB (CIELAB) was the first perceptually uniform model — equal numerical steps produce equal perceived differences — but its cylindrical child LCH is more practical: Lightness (0–100), Chroma (unbounded), Hue (0–360°). OKLCH refines LCH with better hue linearity and is the current best choice for CSS work [evilmartians, "OKLCH in CSS: why we quit RGB and HSL"].

**2. OKLCH is the practical standard for modern UI color work.**
`oklch(L C H / alpha)` is supported in all major browsers since Firefox 113, Chrome 111, and Safari 16.4. Its perceptually uniform lightness means that adjusting only hue produces colors that appear equally bright — this is impossible with HSL. Shade ramps generated by stepping `L` uniformly in OKLCH have consistent perceived contrast, making WCAG compliance predictable. The CSS syntax is `oklch(0.65 0.15 260)` for a mid-blue; relative color syntax `oklch(from var(--base) l c calc(h + 120))` generates triadic partners automatically [web.dev, "Color themes with Baseline CSS features"; evilmartians, "Exploring the OKLCH ecosystem"].

**3. Build palettes in three tiers: primitive → semantic → component.**
Primitive colors are numbered scales (50–950 or 100–900, 9–11 steps) covering every hue in the system. Semantic tokens map primitives to intent: `--color-error` = `--red-700`, `--color-surface-elevated` = `--gray-100`. Component tokens reference semantic tokens. This separation lets a single change to `--color-primary` cascade everywhere without hunting through component code. Refactoring UI recommends 8–10 shades per hue; the darkest (900) serves text, the lightest (50/100) serves tinted backgrounds [Refactoring UI, "Building Your Color Palette," 2019].

**4. Semantic palette: primary, neutral, and functional colors each have a distinct role.**
Primary covers brand-identifying actions — buttons, links, selected states. Neutral (grays) covers backgrounds, borders, secondary text, and dominates 60–80% of the visual surface. Functional colors carry universal meaning: red = error/destructive, green = success, amber = warning, blue = informational. WCAG Success Criterion 1.4.1 requires these meanings never be conveyed by color alone — pair every status color with an icon, text label, or pattern [WCAG 2.1, SC 1.4.1, Level A; NN/g, "Using Color to Enhance Your Design"].

**5. Tint your neutrals toward the brand hue.**
Pure machine-gray (`hsl(0 0% 50%)`) reads as cold and disconnected. A slight hue push — typically 3–8% chroma in OKLCH, or a hue angle matching the primary — creates warmth or coolness that coheres with the brand. In OKLCH this is trivial: set `C` to `0.02–0.06` and `H` to match the primary's hue angle. The result is neutrals that feel designed rather than defaulted [Dennis Cortés, "How to Pick the Grays in Your Design System"; evilmartians OKLCH palette tools].

**6. The 60-30-10 rule governs proportional color distribution.**
Borrowed from interior design: 60% dominant (backgrounds, large surfaces — usually a neutral), 30% secondary (sidebars, card fills, secondary buttons), 10% accent (CTAs, highlights, interactive states). The accent is the most saturated and most attention-commanding color, so restricting it to 10% keeps hierarchy intact. In OKLCH terms, the accent can carry high chroma (`C > 0.15`) because it appears rarely; the dominant neutral should sit at near-zero chroma [NN/g, "Using Color to Enhance Your Design"; UX Planet, "The 60-30-10 Rule in UI Design"].

**7. Color harmony schemes provide principled multi-hue palettes.**
The four most useful for UI: *Complementary* — hues 180° apart, maximum contrast, strong CTA pop (e.g. blue brand + orange accent). *Analogous* — hues 30–60° apart, cohesive and calm, often needs an external accent to create hierarchy. *Triadic* — hues 120° apart, vibrant and balanced, suits products that need energy without aggression. *Split-complementary* — base hue plus two colors flanking its complement (~150° and 210°), retains tension while reducing visual harshness. In CSS with relative color syntax, these relationships are arithmetic: `calc(h + 180)`, `calc(h + 120)`, etc. [Supercharge Design, "Color Harmonies in UI"; web.dev color themes article].

**8. Shade ramps must be perceptually even, not mathematically even.**
Stepping `L` in equal intervals in OKLCH (e.g. 0.95, 0.85, 0.72, 0.60, 0.48, 0.36, 0.24, 0.12) produces ramps that look evenly spaced because the model is perceptually uniform. The same step in HSL (e.g. 90%, 80%, 70% L) does not — yellow lightness steps look different from blue lightness steps at the same percentages. Refactoring UI's manual-judgment approach (fill in gaps, trust your eyes) is a pragmatic fallback for legacy tooling; OKLCH makes the mathematical approach reliable [evilmartians OKLCH; atmos.style, "LCH is the best color space for UI"].

**9. WCAG contrast ratios are the legal and ethical floor.**
WCAG 2.1 Level AA (the de facto global standard) requires: normal text ≥ 4.5:1, large text (≥18pt or ≥14pt bold) ≥ 3:1, non-text UI components ≥ 3:1 (SC 1.4.11). Level AAA raises normal text to 7:1. Contrast ratios cannot be rounded — 4.47:1 fails 4.5:1. All interactive states (hover, focus, disabled) must independently meet the threshold. Text over gradients or semi-transparent overlays must be tested at every point along the gradient [WebAIM, "Contrast and Color Accessibility"; WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.3, 1.4.6, 1.4.11].

**10. Color semantics carry cultural freight — never assume universality.**
Red signals danger in Western contexts but luck and celebration in Chinese culture. White means purity in Western contexts but mourning in parts of East Asia. Green means success in most UI conventions but carries nationalistic associations that exclude it from certain markets. Blue is the closest thing to a universal "safe" color for primary actions. Design for a specific audience first; for global products, rely on secondary cues (icons, text) so meaning survives across cultural readings [cieden.com, "Color Symbolism and Meanings"; NN/g color guidelines].

**11. Dark mode requires elevation-by-lightness, not color inversion.**
Material Design specifies `#121212` (not `#000000`) as the recommended dark surface base — pure black eliminates depth cues and causes harsh irradiation with white text. Elevation in dark mode is expressed by lightening surfaces: base background ~L 8–10%, cards ~L 14–17%, modals ~L 20–24%, tooltips ~L 26–30% in OKLCH. Shadows work less well in dark mode than in light mode; surface lightness becomes the primary depth signal. Desaturate accent colors in dark mode — fully saturated hues vibrate against dark backgrounds and cause eye strain [Material Design, "Dark Theme"; Uxcel, "Mastering Elevation for Dark UI"].

**12. Accessible color design uses redundant channels — never hue alone.**
Approximately 8% of men and 0.5% of women have color vision deficiency, most commonly red-green (deuteranopia/protanopia). WCAG SC 1.4.1 (Level A) explicitly prohibits using color as the sole means of conveying information. Pair every hue signal with a second channel: shape, icon, text label, position, or pattern. Error states need an icon or "Error:" label, not just a red border. Charts need different line styles or data labels in addition to different colors. Test designs with simulation tools such as Chrome DevTools' vision deficiency emulator [WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.1; WebAIM contrast guidance; NN/g accessibility guidelines].

## How to apply (web UI)

- **DO** define your entire palette as OKLCH custom properties at `:root` — `--blue-600: oklch(0.55 0.18 260)` — so every consumer gets the perceptual model.
- **DO** use `light-dark(var(--surface-light), var(--surface-dark))` with `color-scheme: light dark` on `:root` for automatic mode switching without duplicating media queries.
- **DO** generate shade ramps by stepping `L` in OKLCH (e.g. from `0.97` to `0.12` in 9 uniform steps) and verifying each step against the next with a contrast checker.
- **DO** tint your gray scale: set chroma to `0.02–0.05` at the same hue as your primary — `oklch(0.97 0.03 260)` for a cool near-white that reads as designed.
- **DO** use `color-mix(in oklch, var(--primary) 15%, white)` for hover/focus tints — perceptually predictable results.
- **DO** check all text contrast programmatically in CI using a tool like `@accessibility-checker/core` before merge.
- **DO** pair every status color with an icon: `<ErrorIcon aria-hidden="true" /> Error: message`.
- **DO** keep accent color usage to ~10% of the visual surface; use the neutral scale for everything else.
- **AVOID** building shade ramps in HSL — `hsl(220 70% 50%)` and `hsl(60 70% 50%)` look nothing like the same lightness.
- **AVOID** `darken()`/`lighten()` Sass functions in production — they generate hundreds of unregistered one-off values.
- **AVOID** using pure `#000` for text or `#fff` for backgrounds — use `oklch(0.10 0.01 260)` and `oklch(0.98 0.01 260)` instead.
- **AVOID** reversing a light-mode palette directly to dark mode — inversion does not preserve elevation semantics.
- **AVOID** fully saturated accents in dark mode — drop chroma by 20–30% relative to light-mode values.

## Anti-patterns

**Gray text on saturated colored backgrounds.** Mid-gray text (`#888`) on a blue or green card background routinely fails 4.5:1. Always measure contrast against the actual rendered background, not the assumed sRGB value — use a browser DevTools eyedropper.

**Pure `#000000` text and `#ffffff` backgrounds.** The harshness of maximum contrast causes irradiation and eye strain during extended reading. Use near-black (`oklch(0.10 0.01 260)`) on near-white (`oklch(0.98 0.01 260)`) — still well above 4.5:1 but far more comfortable.

**Hue-only signaling.** Red border for errors, green border for success — without icon or label — fails WCAG SC 1.4.1 and is completely invisible to ~8% of male users with red-green color blindness.

**Equal-saturation palettes.** Assigning all palette hues the same chroma (`C`) value looks inconsistent because chroma is not perceptually uniform across hues. Yellow at `C = 0.18` is overwhelmingly vivid; blue at `C = 0.18` is moderate. In OKLCH, calibrate each hue's max chroma by eye or against the sRGB gamut boundary.

**Direct dark-mode inversion.** Swapping `background: white → black` and `color: black → white` removes all depth hierarchy and forces the eye to fight maximum contrast. Dark mode requires a purposefully designed separate scale, not `filter: invert(1)`.

**Unlimited accent use.** Using the primary brand color at full saturation for backgrounds, borders, fills, buttons, and icons simultaneously destroys hierarchy — the eye has no signal about what matters most. Restrict high-chroma color to interactive elements and key status callouts.
